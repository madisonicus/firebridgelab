Costs, inflation, pensions and survivor
How these work
ⓘ
Investment cost comes off every year’s return. With pensions fixed until they start, each pension amount is read as the dollar check at its start age and deflated to today at the assumed inflation. The FERS-style adjustment then trails inflation each year it’s paid. A first death keeps the larger Social Security benefit, continues the chosen share of the decedent’s pension, scales spending, and switches federal tax to single-filer brackets.
Guardrail rule (Guyton-Klinger)
Ceiling vs floor tradeoff
ⓘ
Lowering the ceiling caps the upside — the median spend stops rising sooner in strong markets. Raising the floor lifts the worst case but leaves less for the bridge years.
Amortization Frontier
What this dial does
ⓘ
The frontier is the most you could spend each year if you drew the portfolio down to zero by the end of the plan. This is the conservative real return it assumes while doing that math. Drag it up toward your allocation's expected return and the frontier rises — but the late-life downside fan craters, because you'd be spending against returns you might not get. The $85k floor and $150k ceiling are drawn as reference lines only; the frontier is free to cross them.

Under guardrails, spending bends with the market: a bad sequence forces cuts toward the floor, a good one allows raises toward the ceiling. The band shows the 10th–90th percentile of actual spending at each age across every historical cohort.
Honest limit: the upside only spends down surplus up to the ceiling — in strong markets you still finish with money you never spent.
ⓘ
Honest limit: the upside only spends down surplus up to the ceiling. In genuinely strong markets you hit the satiation cap and still finish with money you never spent — a year can only absorb so much quality of life, so “drive the ending value to zero” is bounded by the ceiling, not unlimited.
